D2C for makers

Made it for others? Sell it as your own brand

RS
Raj Shah
Nov 4, 2026 · 6 min read

If you manufacture, export or wholesale for other brands, you already own the hardest part of a D2C business. What you are missing is the part that creates demand.

What you already have

  • Production capacity and quality control
  • Cost advantage from controlling the source
  • Deep product knowledge
  • Existing relationships with buyers and suppliers

A new D2C brand starts without any of this. You start with all of it.

What you are missing

  • A brand: a name, a look and a promise that a customer can remember.
  • Demand: a way to reach shoppers and turn them into repeat buyers.
  • Data: who buys, why, and what they say afterwards. Wholesale hides this from you.
  • A store and fulfilment built for individual orders, not pallets.

Three ways in

  1. Test on a marketplace. Fast and low-effort, but you do not own the customer or the data.
  2. Launch a Shopify store. Slower to start, but you own the brand, the margin and the customer list.
  3. Do both. Use a marketplace to test products and learn, then move buyers to your own store.

Most makers should start with the third path: learn on the marketplace, and build the brand on a store you control.

Why margin changes

The figures below are illustrative, to show the structure, not a benchmark.

  • Price per unit: wholesale is low, set by the buyer; your own D2C brand is the retail price you set.
  • Marketing cost: wholesale is zero; D2C is real, and rising with scale.
  • Volume: wholesale is large and predictable; D2C is smaller, then growing.
  • Customer data: wholesale gives you none; in D2C it is yours.
  • Risk: wholesale is concentrated in a few buyers; D2C is spread across many customers.

D2C is not automatically more profitable. It trades a thin, steady margin for a thicker, riskier one. The trade is worth it when you control cost and can build repeat customers.

The part people avoid: channel conflict

If you make products for other brands, launching your own can upset those buyers. Decide early how to handle it: a clearly different product line, a different price tier, or an honest conversation with your key clients. Silence is the worst option.

A realistic first 90 days

  • Days 1 to 30: pick one product with clear demand and strong margin. Define who it is for. Build the name, look and product pages.
  • Days 31 to 60: launch a store. Run small paid tests and creator content. Collect reviews.
  • Days 61 to 90: double down on what works. Set up retention flows so first-time buyers come back.

Common mistakes

  • Launching a full catalogue instead of one hero product
  • Copying the factory's language instead of speaking to the customer
  • Skipping tracking, so you cannot tell what works
  • Pricing against marketplace discounts instead of your brand's value
  • Treating a store as a catalogue rather than a sales machine
